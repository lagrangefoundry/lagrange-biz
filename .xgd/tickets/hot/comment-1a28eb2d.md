#### Claude
Your read on the legal side is right, and I'd push it one step further: **AI-generating the same story doesn't launder it.** Marvel holds copyright on the Iron Man character and trademarks/trade dress on the armor design. A generated shot of a red-and-gold flying armored suit leaving a garage is arguably *more* exposed than a licensed clip, because it reads as intentional appropriation rather than commentary. Fair use is weakest exactly where you are: commercial, promotional, non-transformative, and trading on the source's appeal.

But here's the thing — the Marvel footage isn't what makes your script good. The **beat structure** is, and that's free:

1. Constraint ("one person, a garage")
2. Tool reveal (the helpers)
3. Capability multiplication ("pretty much anything")
4. Transcendence (the launch)

Nobody owns that. Keep the voiceover verbatim if you want; swap the imagery.

## Three replacements for the launch beat

**A. The pull-back.** Open tight on one person at a workbench in a garage. Voiceover runs. On "pretty much anything," camera pulls back continuously — the garage wall turns out to be one bay of an enormous working shipyard/factory, dozens of stations running in parallel, all unattended. Still one person, now on a mezzanine. Card: *Same person. Same garage. Different tools.*

This is my pick. The pull-back does what the sky-launch does (scale, awe) but it says **throughput**, which is what you're actually selling. Iron Man's flight says *power*; a dev tool buyer wants *leverage*.

**B. The crew of hands.** One pair of hands on a keyboard. Then shadows/reflections of a dozen more pairs working the same bench in parallel, slightly out of sync. Lands the agent-fleet concept literally, and it's a striking image nobody owns.

**C. Real garages, real lineage.** Wright brothers' bicycle shop → a 1970s workbench → a modern desk. Pre-1931 US works are public domain now, so early aviation photography is usable, but verify each asset individually — restorations and reconstructions carry fresh claims, and "it's old" is not a clearance.

## Safer cultural references

The ones that work are **archetypes and real history**, not owned characters: the Da Vinci workshop, the forge, the luthier, the shipwright, the barn-raising, mission control. The "suit-up montage" and "mecha hangar" are genre conventions — you can evoke the *rhythm* freely; you just can't reproduce a specific design.

Two more things worth flagging:

- **Music is the bigger trap than visuals.** Trailer music gets brands sued more often than footage does. Budget for Musicbed/Artlist/Epidemic, and don't let anyone hand you a "sounds like Zimmer" temp track that quietly ships.
- **Your best asset may be real capture.** For a developer tool, actual terminal footage of a fleet of agents working in parallel *is* the robot-helpers shot. It's free, it's yours, and it's more credible than any generated imagery — devs discount stock/AI visuals hard, and the thing itself is the proof.

#### Claude
Right — dropping IP entirely. The real problem with the draft isn't the envelope, it's that **the message is the category's message, not yours.**

"With the right tools, one person can do anything" is what every AI dev tool is saying right now. Copilot, Cursor, every agent startup. If you run that message, you're competing on brand recognition and budget, and you lose. Worse: your audience has *already heard it* and been burned — they got the fast demo and then got unmaintainable slop.

## Your four archetypes aren't interchangeable

Each makes a different promise, and that's the actual decision in front of you:

| Archetype | What it promises |
|---|---|
| Wizard in a workshop | Mastery. Arcane, earned, deep. *You become formidable.* |
| Doc Brown / Rick | Chaotic genius. *Wild results, hold on tight.* |
| Kid with a chemistry set | Accessibility, wonder. *Anyone can start.* |
| Witch brewing potions | Craft, recipe, repeatability. *There is a method.* |

Doc Brown actively fights your product. His whole trope is *brilliance without control* — the thing goes wrong, the garage catches fire, that's the joke. That is precisely the anxiety your buyer already has about AI agents. The kid-with-chem-set is a different funnel entirely (wonder, top-of-funnel). The wizard and the witch are the two that fit, and they fit for the same reason: both imply **a discipline, not a gift**.

## The message I'd actually buy

Not *one person becomes a superhero*. One person becomes **an engineering organization**.

That's the honest description of what XGD is — tickets, branches, review gates, regression, a capability matrix. That's an org chart expressed as software. The promise isn't "you're 10x faster," it's "you have the apparatus a team has: process, standards, someone checking the work." Superhero framing says *amplified individual*. Organization framing says *institution of one* — and nobody else in the category is claiming that, because most of them can't.

So: **"What can one person do in their garage?" → "Run an engineering department."**

## The structural move that makes it land

Every wizard/witch/mad-scientist story has the same third beat: **the experiment gets away from you.** The potion boils over. The brooms multiply and flood the room. Your audience *already has* that story loaded as their mental model for AI agents — uncontrolled multiplication, chaos, mess.

Competitors' ads stop at beat two — *look, the helpers appeared!* Yours should run the turn and then not pay it off:

1. **The ambition.** One person. Something absurdly out of scale for one person.
2. **The summoning.** The helpers arrive. Many of them. (What everyone shows.)
3. **The turn.** The moment it should go wrong. Build the dread — the audience knows this beat, they're waiting for the flood.
4. **It holds.** Something checks the work. Something catches the bad batch. The chaos is *contained* — and that containment is the product.
5. **The scale reveal.** Because it held, it kept going, and the output is enormous.

Beat 3 is where you win. It says *we know what you're afraid of and we built for it*. Beat 4 is the only beat that's uniquely yours.

## Line work

Your VO has two soft spots. "**Pretty much** anything" hedges at the exact moment you should be certain. And "the right tools" credits tools generically — tools are what competitors sell. You sell the workshop.

Tagline directions, in order of how much I like them:

- **"The workshop, not the wand."**
- **"Everyone can summon help. XGD makes it hold."**
- **"One person. A full workshop."**
- **"The magic is the discipline."**

"Tooling to take engineers to the next level" should go — "next level" is filler that could sit under any logo in the category.

## The one thing that decides the rest

Is this **awareness** (get people to feel the possibility) or **differentiation** (why XGD and not the agent tool they're already using)?

If awareness: the kid with the chemistry set, keep it wondrous, skip the turn.

If differentiation — and given you're selling a product, I think it is — run the wizard/witch, and **the turn is the whole trailer**.

Tell me which, and I'll write the beat sheet with the VO locked.

#### Claude
Yes. And it maps tighter than you probably realize — the details of the fable line up with your architecture almost line for line.

**The apprentice's actual failure was that he only learned half the spell.** He knew the summoning. He didn't know the word that stops it. That's the entire AI coding market right now: generation is solved and universally available; termination, bounds, and verification are not. XGD is the second half of the spell.

The rest of the mapping:

- He chops the broom in half — **it becomes two brooms.** Uncontrolled parallelism, agents spawning agents.
- The water never stops coming — **no exit condition.**
- He can't tell progress from flood — **no verification, no matrix.**
- And the one that's genuinely chilling: **the brooms were never wrong. They were obedient.** They did exactly what he said, forever. That is the actual AI failure mode, not hallucination — faithful execution of an under-specified instruction at machine scale. If you land one line in this trailer, land that one.

## The trap, and it's a real one

If XGD is *the master who returns and says the word*, then your customer is the apprentice — the kid who flailed and flooded the room. You've just told a room full of proud senior engineers that they're the incompetent one and you're the adult. That will not sell.

**Invert it: XGD makes them the sorcerer.** The apprentice is who you are *without* it — or more pointedly, who the other tools leave you as. Your buyer isn't being rescued; they're being handed the word of command. Same fable, opposite ego position, and it costs you nothing structurally.

## The resolution can't be magic

Here's the discipline this concept demands. You're going to spend the first twenty seconds vividly amplifying your category's single biggest objection. That's a bold, good move — but it only works if the answer is **concrete and legible**. If the payoff is a swoosh and a logo, you have made an extremely effective anti-AI ad and paid for it yourself.

So: the flood is the spectacle. **The fix should be quiet, specific, and mundane.** Something visibly *checks* the work and catches the bad batch. That tonal contrast — chaos answered by something almost boring — *is* the brand. Dry and confident, not comic. Comedy about your buyer's pain point reads as mockery.

## Don't end on "the flood stops"

This is the part I'd push hardest. Stopping is defensive. It frames XGD as insurance, and insurance is a grudge purchase — nobody gets excited about the thing that prevents a bad day.

End instead on: **the brooms keep working, and the water goes where it was supposed to go.** The fleet doesn't shrink. The scale is *retained*, the chaos is removed. Because the real claim is:

> **Control isn't the opposite of scale. It's the precondition for it.**

Without verification you have to stop at three agents, because that's all you can personally check. With it you can run fifty. Discipline is a *throughput* feature, not a safety feature — and that reframe is the thing none of your competitors can say, because most of them haven't built it.

## Beat sheet

Picking this fable answered my earlier question, by the way — you've chosen differentiation. The turn is the trailer.

1. **The ambition.** One person, something absurdly out of scale for one person.
2. **The summoning.** It works. Helpers everywhere. Genuine delight. *(Where every competitor's ad ends.)*
3. **The turn.** It doesn't stop. It multiplies. Dread builds — the audience knows this story and is bracing for the flood. *"They weren't wrong. They were obedient."*
4. **The word.** Something checks. Something catches. Quiet, precise, unglamorous.
5. **The retained scale.** The fleet is still running — bigger than before — and every output is landing where it should. Card.

The fable is Goethe, 1797, and the broom-and-flood imagery long predates the version everyone pictures — so you have room to build something original on it. But that's the envelope, and you wanted the message.
